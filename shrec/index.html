Part one of two

What the evidence says

Three findings, in order. One about what children need from us. One about what predicts whether they get it. And one, more recent and more uncomfortable, about who receives it now.

Evidence · one of three

Quality of exchange, not volume of talk

Children benefit from responsive, socially meaningful, back-and-forth exchanges, not simply from hearing more words. That distinction does most of the work in everything that follows.

EEF, 2018

First among equals

Preparing for Literacy places the development of communication and language first among its recommendations for the early years.

Ang et al., 2025

Counting is not the point

Approaches that tally questions, vocabulary or talk opportunities can miss whether the adult was genuinely attuned to the child at all.

EEF, 2018

Harder than it looks

High-quality interactions can look effortless. Doing them well is complex, demanding, and takes real skill from the educator.

Evidence · two of three

“Conversational turns are one of the strongest predictors of children's linguistic, cognitive, and neural development”

Romeo and colleagues found it was the turns, not the adult word count, that predicted activation in the brain's language regions. That held independent of family income.

Romeo, R. et al. (2021) ‘Neuroplasticity associated with changes in conversational turn-taking following a family-based intervention’, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience, 49, 100967. Earlier finding: Romeo et al. (2018), Psychological Science.

Evidence · three of three

Attention is already unequal. The question is which way.

Duthois et al., 2026

Who gets overlooked

Attention was distributed unevenly. The children receiving least were those with low speaking confidence, lower language proficiency, and those learning English alongside another language.

Duthois et al., 2025

The talkative child

In a single-classroom study, the most talkative child drew the most teacher attention, and so received the most opportunity to develop language.

REPEY, 2002

Not a new finding

Siraj-Blatchford and colleagues found children seen as struggling drew the highest share of behaviour management and a smaller share of social talk.

Four moves you already make

Sh · Share attention

Follow the child's gaze before you follow your plan

This is

What it looks like

Positioned at the child's level. Tuned in to what they are focused on, noticing what they look at, what they do and what they say. Body language and face showing genuine interest.

This is not

The near miss

Disrupting or diverting the child's focus in order to gain their attention. The child chooses the subject. The adult sustains it.

Worth saying

Not standing back

Shared attention is active participation. Child-led practice has sometimes been read as the adult withdrawing, which is not the same thing at all.

R · Respond

The child's contribution decides yours

Non-verbal

Before a word

Eye contact, looking expectantly, nodding, smiling. Tone of voice, face and body carrying warmth and interest.

Verbal

Narrate and comment

Say what the child is doing. Comment on what they can see, hear or feel. The commentary is the invitation.

This is not

Steering

Using verbal and non-verbal strategies to draw out a specific response, or to change what the child is doing.

Responsiveness comes before vocabulary. Notice first, extend second.

E · Expand

One step beyond, not five

“red car”
“the red car”
“the red car is going fast”
“the red car went past the gate”
This is

Pitched just above

Repeat and build on what the child said, turning it into a phrase or a sentence. Recast pronunciation and grammar without comment. Follow the child's lead.

This is not

Echoing, or correcting

Repeating the child's words back with the same vocabulary adds nothing. Neither does stopping the exchange to fix it.

C · Conversation

Comment more, question less

“The goal is to develop sustained back-and-forth conversations that involve many turns.”

Pause

Leave the gap

Time to listen, time to process, time to reply. The silence after your comment is doing work.

Sustain

Respond and expand again

The two previous moves come back, as often as the exchange needs them, to keep the turns coming.

Reach

Beyond the here and now

Yesterday, tomorrow, the thing that is not in the room. New words arrive naturally when the talk travels.

Implementation

What can change, and what cannot

A seatbelt should reduce injury in a crash. Its core components are proper restraint across the body, and tension that locks under sudden deceleration.

Lethal mutation

Worn loosely

Slack across the chest, or tucked behind the back. It still looks like a seatbelt. The function that produced the benefit has gone.

Intelligent adaptation

Adjusted to fit

Different colours, adjustable height, a child version and an adult version. The function survives the change.

So

Agree it together

Building a shared understanding of core components lets a team decide what can and cannot be adapted. That is what makes implementation hold.

Core components

What is in your workbook

A seatbelt only works if everyone agrees which part is load-bearing. The same goes for the four moves, and agreeing it starts with each of you knowing honestly where you stand on each one.

Yours to keep

The workbook

The four moves and what each one looks like, the prompts and cues pages, both activities we do later, and the goal-setting columns. It goes back to your room with you, not back to me.

Activity · with your team

Core components self-reflection

Take each of the four moves in turn. Confident, or getting there, or wanting more practice and support? Honest answers make the next observation useful rather than performative.

Not today, and not on your own. This is the one worth having the whole team in the room for.

Guarding the approach

What ShREC is, and what it is not

It is not

An intervention
There is no group, no entry criteria, no exit data.
A slot in the timetable
There is no such thing as ShREC time.
A silver bullet
It sits alongside everything else you do, not above it.
A script
A distressed child needs reassurance, not a conversation. Attunement outranks fidelity, always.

It is

A shared language
Names something skilled educators already do, so it can be discussed, coached and improved.
A guiding principle
For professional development, observation and professional conversation across the trust.
Portable
Snack table, sand tray, the walk to the gate. Four moves that travel across the whole day.
Deliberately small
The original programme had five strands and practitioners found it daunting. Cutting it to four was the implementation strategy.
Equity

Who needs a bigger slice of cake?

same room
different shares

Talkative · confident · quieter · learning English alongside another language

Attention is finite and it never divides evenly. Deliberate is not the same as equal. The child who says least often has the most waiting to be said.

Embedding practice

Prompts and cues in the room

The EEF names prompts and cues among the mechanisms that make professional development stick. The room can carry some of the load your memory currently carries.

Space

Where talk happens

Which areas are popular and well used, and which are left unused? Which encourage conversation? Where can a child be quieter?

Resources

What invites talk

Provision that gives children something worth commenting on. Inside and outside both count, and outside is often where the best turns happen.

Reminders

Small and visible

The four moves at adult eye level. One move on the lanyard for a fortnight. A prompt where the interaction actually happens, not in the staff room.

Activity · 15 minutes · workbook

Identifying talking hot spots

Three steps

Step one
On plain paper, draw the layout of your setting. Inside and outside.
Step two
Think about where talking takes place. Child-initiated, adult-initiated, child to child, and children talking to themselves.
Step three
In red, mark a cross at each talking hot spot. One cross where talk happens sometimes, more crosses where it happens often. In blue, circle the missed opportunities.

Then

Compare
Swap maps with someone who works in a different part of the setting. The disagreements are the interesting part.
Notice
Blue circles are not failures. They are the cheapest available opportunity you have.
Keep it
The map goes in the workbook and comes back out at the follow-up conversation.

Activity · 10 minutes · workbook

Do your routines support communication?

The activities you plan, the routines you follow and the way the setting is set up all decide how much talk is possible before anyone opens their mouth.

Three steps

Step one
Draw a timeline of a typical day in your setting, start to finish. Include the parts nobody plans.
Step two
In red, highlight where good talking already happens. Adult to child and child to child both count.
Step three
In blue, mark the times when talk could happen and does not yet. Red first, then blue. You cannot see the gap until you have marked what is already there.

Then

Compare
Swap timelines with someone who works a different part of the day. The handover is where the two versions stop matching.
Notice
Blue tends to cluster in the same places. Transitions, tidy-up, handwashing, waiting at the door. The cheapest minutes in the day to change, because nobody has to plan anything to get them.
Keep it
The timeline goes in the workbook next to your map, and comes back out at the follow-up conversation.
Goal setting

The professional development conversation cycle

One goal
1 · prepare, reflect and plan
2 · build knowledge and motivate
3 · identify ways to develop practice
4 · plan to embed and review
5 · agree new goals

Training on its own does not change practice. The cycle is what turns a good morning into a different September.

Activity · 15 minutes · workbook

Goal setting action plan

Two versions in your workbook. Same five columns. Choose the one that fits how your team actually works.

Personal

My own interactions

What do I want to upgrade, naming the specific core component? What will build my knowledge? How will I develop the practice, and who will watch me do it? When do we review?

Collaborative

Our team, our cohort

Start from the needs of this cohort. Which components will move these children? What can we watch together, rehearse together, and come back to as a team?

One goal. Small enough that it survives a difficult morning.
